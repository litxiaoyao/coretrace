\begin{abstract}
Language models can answer each hop of a factual chain from the same evidence yet fail to compose the answers. We study this gap through \emph{Locate--Route--Read}: locating the bridge evidence, routing attention to it, and reading the requested relation value. Across three models, composition fails on roughly half of the sources with correct constituent answers. Paired-query profiles identify evidence-access heads that shift their preference between root and bridge paragraphs; removing these heads disrupts answering. Bridge addresses nevertheless remain recoverable in most composition failures. Matching address readouts to routing interventions connects this available information to corrections on the same sources. We then factor paragraph attention into access mass and its conditional token distribution. Crossed interventions show that routing changes the utility of local reading patterns, with interaction estimates of 5--16 percentage points and receiver-level corrections unique to the joint intervention. Guided by this mechanism, \method\ combines selected-head adaptation with attention-guided evidence selection. It improves composed accuracy by 33.3--39.9 percentage points with fewer than 1\% trainable model parameters, and reciprocal fact exchanges show that repaired predictions follow changed local values. These findings connect component function, evidence access, and relation reading into a mechanistic account of contextual composition.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work studies how language models use supplied evidence and develops targeted interventions for more reliable contextual question answering. Its findings can inform systems that connect facts across documents. We evaluate reasoning and general capabilities to characterize the effects of adaptation. Experiments use public question-answering datasets. Downstream applications should assess evidence quality, answer accuracy, and task-specific reliability under their deployment conditions.

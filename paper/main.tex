\begin{abstract}
Language models can answer both hops of a factual chain from the same evidence yet fail to compose them. We investigate this gap through three functions: locating the bridge, routing attention to it, and reading its requested relation. Across three models, nearly half of sources with correct constituents fail to compose, although bridge addresses remain recoverable in 72.2--86.0\% of these failures. Paired queries localize evidence-access heads whose preference shifts from root to bridge when the intermediate entity is explicit. Factoring paragraph attention into access mass and conditional token weights reveals that routing changes the utility of local reading: joint interventions yield interactions of 5--16 percentage points and recover errors that neither resolves alone. Guided by this mechanism, \method\ adapts the localized heads, restores native prefill projections, and selects evidence using their address signals. With fewer than 1\% trainable parameters, it improves composed accuracy by 33.3--39.9 points. Paired internal measurements reveal increased bridge access and stronger target-relation preference after adaptation and restoration; repaired answers follow exchanged values at stable addresses. These findings connect evidence access, relation-specific reading, and contextual composition.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work studies how language models use supplied evidence and develops targeted interventions for more reliable contextual question answering. Its findings can inform systems that connect facts across documents. We evaluate reasoning and general capabilities to characterize the effects of adaptation. Experiments use public question-answering datasets. Downstream applications should assess evidence quality, answer accuracy, and task-specific reliability under their deployment conditions.

\begin{abstract}
Language models can answer each hop of a factual chain from the same evidence yet fail to compose the answers. We investigate this gap by tracing how a composed question accesses and reads its supporting paragraphs. Paired constituent and composed queries localize a small set of evidence-access heads whose removal sharply reduces answering accuracy. Across three models, composition fails on roughly half of the sources with correct constituent answers, even though the bridge-paragraph address remains recoverable from attention features in most of these failures. We explain this discrepancy by separating paragraph-level attention mass from the conditional attention pattern within the paragraph. Crossed interventions reveal a functional interaction: calibrating access increases the benefit of direct-query reading patterns, with interaction estimates of 5--16 percentage points. This finding connects evidence location to the computation that uses its content. Guided by the localized heads and recoverable addresses, we introduce \method, which combines selected-head adaptation with attention-guided evidence selection. With fewer than 1\% trainable model parameters, it improves composed accuracy by 33.3--39.9 percentage points. Reciprocal fact exchanges further show that its answers track changed local values. Together, these findings identify access-dependent local reading as a mechanism of contextual composition and a practical target for improvement.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work studies how language models use supplied evidence and develops targeted interventions for more reliable contextual question answering. Its findings can inform systems that connect facts across documents. We evaluate reasoning and general capabilities to characterize the effects of adaptation. Experiments use public question-answering datasets. Downstream applications should assess evidence quality, answer accuracy, and task-specific reliability under their deployment conditions.

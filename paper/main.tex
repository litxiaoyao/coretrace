\begin{abstract}
Language models can answer both constituent questions from the same evidence yet fail to compose their answers. Across three models, this happens on roughly half of the cases with correct constituent answers. We identify a gap between recoverable evidence location and effective evidence use: a training-fitted linear readout identifies the bridge paragraph in 72.2--86.0\% of these failed compositions. Factorizing attention into paragraph access mass and within-paragraph reading reveals that access calibration increases the benefit of direct-hop reading patterns, with interaction effects of 5--16 percentage points. After reciprocal fact exchanges preserving global word counts, repaired predictions follow changed local values in 76--80\% of cases, compared with 26--35\% natively. These findings motivate \method, which combines selected-head adaptation, input-derived evidence selection, and fixed projection restoration. On 1,000 contextual reasoning triplets, composed accuracy rises from 26.9/29.2/36.4\% to 66.8/67.2/69.7\% for Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, and Llama-3.1-8B, using fewer than 1\% trainable model parameters. The attention readout resolves ambiguous evidence with an extra input pass on 10.3\% of composed questions. Together, these results connect a causal account of evidence use to an efficient repair of contextual reasoning.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work studies how language models use supplied evidence and develops targeted interventions for more reliable contextual question answering. Its findings can inform systems that connect facts across documents. We evaluate reasoning and general capabilities to characterize the effects of adaptation. Experiments use public question-answering datasets. Downstream applications should assess evidence quality, answer accuracy, and task-specific reliability under their deployment conditions.

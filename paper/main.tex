\begin{abstract}
Language models can answer both hops of a factual chain from the same context yet fail to compose them. Is the intermediate evidence inaccessible, or is it ineffectively used? Across three models, nearly half of sources with correct constituent answers fail to compose, although bridge addresses remain recoverable in 72.2--86.0\% of these failures. We identify shared evidence-access heads and factorize their attention into paragraph-level access mass and conditional within-paragraph reading patterns. Crossed causal interventions reveal that the same relation-specific reading pattern can be ineffective or detrimental at native access yet beneficial when bridge access is strengthened. Joint interventions yield positive interaction estimates of 5--16 percentage points and resolve errors missed by either intervention alone. Guided by this discovery, we introduce \method, which adapts the localized heads and exploits their address signals to focus evidence. With fewer than 1\% trainable model parameters, \method\ improves composed exact-match accuracy by 33.3--39.9 points. Bridge access and relation preference strengthen on unchanged contexts, and repaired answers follow exchanged facts at stable addresses. These findings reveal why locating evidence is not enough: its contribution to composition depends on how access and local reading are coordinated.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work investigates how language models use supplied evidence and develops targeted interventions for contextual question answering. The findings can inform systems that connect facts across documents. Evaluations use public datasets and include reasoning and general capabilities. Downstream applications should assess evidence quality, answer accuracy, and task-specific reliability under their deployment conditions.

\begin{abstract}
Language models can answer both hops of a factual chain from the same evidence yet fail to compose them. We investigate why accessible facts do not always become usable evidence, distinguishing three functions: locating the bridge, routing attention to it, and reading its requested relation. Across three models, nearly half of sources with correct constituent answers still fail in composition, even though bridge addresses remain recoverable in 72.2--86.0\% of these failures. Paired queries reveal evidence-access heads whose preference shifts from root to bridge when the intermediate entity is explicit. Reallocating their attention to the bridge repairs failures, but access is only part of the story. By independently varying paragraph access mass and conditional token weights, we find that routing changes the utility of local reading. Joint interventions yield interaction estimates of 5--16 percentage points and recover errors that neither intervention resolves alone. Guided by these findings, \method\ adapts localized heads and uses their address signals to select evidence, improving composed accuracy by 33.3--39.9 points with fewer than 1\% trainable parameters. Repaired answers follow exchanged relation values at stable evidence addresses. These results reveal coordinated evidence access and reading as an actionable mechanism of contextual composition.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work studies how language models use supplied evidence and develops targeted interventions for more reliable contextual question answering. Its findings can inform systems that connect facts across documents. We evaluate reasoning and general capabilities to characterize the effects of adaptation. Experiments use public question-answering datasets. Downstream applications should assess evidence quality, answer accuracy, and task-specific reliability under their deployment conditions.
